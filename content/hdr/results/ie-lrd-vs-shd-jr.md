**Bottom line.** In 2021 Ireland replaced the controversial Strategic Housing Development fast-track with a new regime called Large-scale Residential Development. Four years on, the honest answer to whether court challenges fell is: we cannot yet tell. The public data does not contain the numbers needed to make a fair comparison, and several other reforms landed at the same time pulling in the same direction.

## The Question

Between 2017 and 2021 Ireland ran a planning regime called Strategic Housing Development — SHD for short. It let big housing schemes skip local councils and apply directly to An Bord Pleanála (ABP), the national planning board. It became notorious because almost every decision that reached the High Court was overturned. In December 2021 it was replaced by Large-scale Residential Development (LRD), which sent those applications back to local councils first. The stated goal was to make the resulting permissions stick.

The obvious follow-up: did the new regime cut the rate at which big housing permissions get dragged into court? If you are a first-time buyer waiting for apartments, or a councillor trying to figure out whether the latest reform is working, this is the number you want.

## What we found

The comparison cannot honestly be made from the data the state currently publishes. Not "the reform did not work", not "the reform worked" — the numbers required to decide are not in any public document available today.

- The only common denominator that lets the two regimes be compared on the same definition puts them within about one percentage point of each other. That proves nothing, because the denominator only counts a subset of the new-regime permissions.
- The count of new-regime court challenges actually decided by the end of 2024 is two. Both were conceded by the planning board rather than fought.
- The overall volume of planning judicial reviews in Ireland jumped 58 percent between 2023 and 2024 — with commercial challenges up 150 percent and infrastructure challenges up 850 percent. These movements have nothing to do with the housing reform.
- Three other legal changes landed in roughly the same window (a Supreme Court ruling on costs, a new specialist court list, and a shift in the planning board's own defensive strategy) that each pull the numbers in the same direction the reform intended.
- A defensible answer needs at least two more years of data and a dataset from the Department of Housing that has never been published.

## Why that matters

The usual political framing of a reform like this is binary — it worked, or it did not, and the press wants an answer inside 18 months. The real picture is that you need five to six years of post-reform data, plus a departmental dataset that has never been released, before the question is even answerable. Anything faster than that is guessing.

It is also a reminder that reforms rarely land alone. The new housing regime, a court-costs ruling, a new fast-tracked court list, and a change in the planning board's legal posture all began producing effects in roughly the same 18-month window. Separating their individual contributions at annual resolution is genuinely impossible with current data — not merely difficult.

## What it means in practice

**For the housing debate.** The honest position is humility. The reform may be working, may be failing, or may be indistinguishable from the other legal changes happening at the same time. Anyone offering a confident answer is guessing.

**For data transparency.** The specific missing piece matters. The Department of Housing holds counts of how many large-scale residential permissions local councils issued in 2022, 2023, and 2024. Publishing them would turn an unanswerable question into an answerable one. Until that happens, the public debate is arguing about a number nobody can calculate.

## How we did it

The analysis draws on publicly available [An Bord Pleanála annual reports](https://www.pleanala.ie/en-ie/publications) from 2020 to 2024, the Office of the Planning Regulator's schedule of decided judicial reviews, and two Learning-from-Litigation bulletins that cover more recent High Court cases. Case records were parsed from the source PDFs and cross-checked against a hand-verified ground truth. Where a fair comparison required data that is not in any public document, the question was marked unanswerable rather than patched with an incompatible number.
