## The Question

Between 2017 and 2021 Ireland ran a planning regime called Strategic Housing Development, which let big housing schemes skip local councils and go straight to the national planning board. It became notorious because almost every decision that reached the High Court was overturned. In December 2021 it was replaced by a new regime, Large-scale Residential Development, which sent those applications back to local councils first. The stated goal was to make the resulting permissions stick.

The obvious follow-up question, four years later: did the new regime actually cut the rate at which big housing permissions get dragged into court? If you are a first-time buyer waiting for apartments to be built, or a councillor trying to figure out whether the latest reform is working, this is the number you want.

## What We Found

The short answer is that the comparison cannot honestly be made from the data the state currently publishes. Not "the reform didn't work", not "the reform worked" — the numbers required to decide are not in any public document available today.

- The only common denominator that lets the two regimes be compared on the same definition puts them within about one percentage point of each other — which proves nothing, because that denominator only counts a subset of the new-regime permissions.
- The count of new-regime court challenges that have actually been decided to the end of 2024 is two. Both were conceded by the planning board rather than fought.
- The overall volume of planning judicial reviews in Ireland jumped 58 percent between 2023 and 2024, with commercial challenges up 150 percent and infrastructure challenges up 850 percent — movements that have nothing to do with the housing reform.
- Three other legal changes landed around the same time (a Supreme Court ruling on costs, a new specialist court list, and a shift in the planning board's own defensive strategy) that each pull the numbers in the same direction the reform was meant to.
- A defensible answer needs at least two more years of data and a dataset from the Department of Housing that has never been published.

## Why That's Surprising

The usual political framing of this kind of reform is a binary: it either worked or it didn't, and the press wants an answer inside 18 months. The actual picture is that you need five to six years of post-reform data, plus a dataset the relevant department has simply never released, before the question is even answerable. Everything faster than that is guessing.

It is also a reminder that policy reforms rarely land alone. The new housing regime, a court-costs ruling, a new fast-tracked court list, and a change in the planning board's legal posture all began producing effects in roughly the same 18-month window. Separating their individual contributions at annual resolution is genuinely impossible with current data — not merely difficult.

## What It Means

For the housing debate, the honest position is humility: the reform may be working, may be failing, or may be indistinguishable from the other legal changes happening at the same time. Anyone claiming a confident answer is guessing.

For data transparency, the specific missing piece matters. The Department of Housing holds counts of how many large-scale residential permissions local councils issued in 2022, 2023, and 2024. Publishing them would turn an unanswerable question into an answerable one. Until that happens, the public debate is arguing about a number nobody can calculate.

## How We Did It

The analysis draws on the publicly available [An Bord Pleanála annual reports](https://www.pleanala.ie/en-ie/publications) from 2020 to 2024, the Office of the Planning Regulator's schedule of decided judicial reviews, and two Learning-from-Litigation bulletins that cover more recent High Court cases. Case records were parsed from the source PDFs and cross-checked against a hand-verified ground truth. Where a fair comparison between the two regimes required data that is not in any public document, the relevant question was marked unanswerable rather than patched with an incompatible number.
