**Bottom line.** Government-imposed costs — value added tax, the Part V affordable-housing obligation, development contributions, and regulatory compliance — make up only about one-sixth of the cost of building a new Irish home. Zeroing every one of them would move just three of Ireland's 26 counties across the viability line. The real drivers of the gap are materials, labour, land, and finance.

## The Question

Ireland's housing debate tends to treat the policy-imposed part of a new home's price tag as the obvious target for reform. Value added tax at 13.5 percent, the Part V rule requiring one unit in five to be handed to the local authority at cost, county-by-county development contributions, building-regulation compliance fees — each gets its own op-ed, and each is frequently blamed for putting otherwise viable schemes underwater.

We took apart the cost of a representative new home in each of Ireland's 26 counties and asked a precise question. How much of the cost does the state actually set, and if all of it were eliminated, how many counties would cross from unviable to viable for private residential development?

## What we found

Policy costs are a minority of the total, and removing them does not solve the problem.

- Policy-set costs — tax, Part V, development contributions, regulatory compliance, planning fees — account for 13 to 17 percent of the full cost of building a new Irish home. The remaining 83 to 87 percent is market cost: materials, labour, land, finance, professional fees, and developer margin.
- At today's prices and costs, zero of 26 counties are viable for speculative private residential development without intervention.
- Zeroing value added tax on new homes — the single largest policy lever — makes three counties viable: Dublin, Wicklow, and Kildare. No other single policy change crosses any county over the line.
- Halving every policy cost produces the same three. Abolishing every policy cost entirely adds one more: Meath.
- If buyers capture half of any policy saving as a lower purchase price — a common finding in tax-incidence research — then zeroing value added tax moves no counties into viability, and abolishing every policy cost moves only three.
- The median gap between a county's costs and its achievable sale price is about EUR 144,000 per home. Total policy cost per home is about EUR 70,000 to 100,000. Policy reform cannot close a gap of that size.

## Why that matters

The most-repeated claim in the Irish housing debate is that government costs are strangling supply. The numbers put this into sharp perspective. The share of the final cost set by the state is real — 15 percent is not small — but it is bounded. A builder in County Longford faces roughly the same policy costs as one in Dublin. What differs between them, and what pushes Longford EUR 226,000 per unit below viability, is everything else: land, labour, materials, finance, and the price the completed home can be sold for.

The second surprise concerns who gets the benefit of a tax cut. The intuitive assumption is that removing a EUR 45,000 VAT bill on a Dublin home reduces the cost by EUR 45,000 and tips previously unviable schemes over the line. The tax-incidence literature says the saving is usually split between builder and buyer — the buyer through a lower asking price, the builder through a wider margin. A roughly half-and-half split, the standard research assumption, wipes out the viability benefit entirely. The policy lever that looks largest on paper is the one most vulnerable to being absorbed by the market.

The third surprise concerns a category usually treated as sacrosanctly "market": the developer margin. A 15 percent margin is the standard for private speculative development. State-led delivery through the Land Development Agency and approved housing bodies routinely operates at 6 to 8 percent or at cost. Compressing the margin from 15 to 10 percent brings Wicklow within a whisker of viability on its own — moving the line by as much as a major tax change. That is a lever, but not a tax or a regulatory lever. It is a delivery-model lever.

## What it means in practice

**For anyone buying or waiting for a new home.** The absence of new homes in your county is probably not caused by the VAT bill or the Part V obligation. In most of the country, the shortfall between cost and sale price is far too large to be closed by any combination of policy changes. The binding constraint is the price of inputs and the price of land relative to what the completed home can be sold for.

**For policymakers.** Closing the viability gap nationally requires a direct subsidy, a structural intervention in market costs, or a delivery-model shift — not a tax package. A national viability-gap fund at the Housing for All delivery target would cost roughly EUR 4.4 billion per year. Alternatively, the state can sidestep private-sector margin through direct or agency-led delivery, where the 15 percent developer margin simply does not apply. Tax reform still has a case in specific markets close to viability and for specific apartment typologies in Dublin — but presenting it as the primary answer overstates what it can do.

**For everyone.** The line between "policy cost" and "market cost" is blurrier than it looks. The developer margin is set by the market, but the state can absorb it through direct delivery. Land price is set by the market, but compulsory-purchase powers can shift it. The state's share of the problem is larger than the sum of its tax rates — and smaller than the total price tag.

## How we did it

We built a full cost stack for four dwelling types (three-bed semi-detached, two-bed apartment, four-bed detached, three-bed terrace) across four location categories and all 26 Republic of Ireland counties. Build costs came from the [Buildcost.ie H1 2025 Construction Cost Guide](https://buildcost.ie/), adjusted from one-off rebuilding rates to multi-unit scheme costs using a standard 0.85 factor. Land costs came from the [Central Statistics Office Residential Zoned Land Prices table (RZLPA02)](https://data.cso.ie/table/RZLPA02). Policy costs came from published local-authority development-contribution schemes, the value added tax rate, Part V obligations, and Society of Chartered Surveyors Ireland compliance-cost surveys. Each component was classified as policy-set or market-set, each county was tested at baseline and under every combination of policy-cost changes, and the results were stress-tested under a 50 percent pass-through assumption that lets half of any policy saving flow to buyers as lower prices. All inputs are real public data; no synthetic figures were used.
