## The Question

The cost of building a home in Ireland has been at the centre of the country's housing debate for a decade. Most coverage assumes that Irish construction costs are among the highest in Europe, and that high costs are a major reason the country is not building enough. The headline number most often cited — Dublin ranked as Europe's fourth most expensive construction market — actually comes from a survey of commercial buildings in city centres, not ordinary homes.

We set out to test the claim directly. Using the harmonised residential-construction price statistics that Europe publishes for every member state, we asked: against comparable European countries, how high are Irish construction costs really, and how fast have they been rising?

## What We Found

Ireland is unremarkable on every measure that can be compared like-for-like.

- On cumulative price growth since 2015, Ireland ranks sixth of ten comparable European countries. Germany, the Netherlands, Austria, Belgium, and Sweden all grew faster.
- Ireland's growth was slower than the European Union average in every period examined except the 2024-2025 recovery.
- On Europe's harmonised price level index for residential buildings — the most robust cross-country yardstick — Ireland scored 99.7 in 2024, with the European Union average set at 100. Germany was 47 percent more expensive. Denmark and Sweden were around 28 percent more expensive.
- Ireland's price trajectory clusters with Belgium and Sweden (moderate growth), not with the fast-growing German, Dutch, and Austrian group.
- Building a 100-square-metre house takes about four times the median Irish household income, compared with more than five times in Germany and nearly five in Denmark.

## Why That's Surprising

The most counterintuitive result involves the gap between how expensive things generally are in Ireland and how expensive construction specifically is. Ireland's general consumer price level is roughly 27 percent above the European average — groceries, restaurants, and services cost noticeably more than in most European countries. Residential construction, on the harmonised European index, comes in at the European average. Construction is one of the only categories in which Ireland is not expensive relative to its neighbours.

The second surprise is how much of Ireland's cost inflation was common European inflation. Every country examined had its price index broken by the same two shocks, in the same quarters: the pandemic supply-chain disruption that peaked in early 2021, and the Ukraine-driven energy shock in the spring of 2022. Ireland was neither earlier nor more violent on either shock. If there were a distinctively Irish cost problem — driven by Irish labour markets, Irish regulation, or the costs of operating on an island — it should show up as a deviation from the European pattern. It does not. Ireland's curve looks like Belgium's, slightly slower than Sweden's.

The third surprise, for anyone following Irish housing policy, is how little of the popular cost narrative survives contact with the cross-country figures. Germany, the Netherlands, and Austria all had faster price growth, all have much higher absolute costs, and all have housing supply problems of their own. If Irish costs were the binding constraint, countries with more binding cost constraints should be building fewer homes, not more.

## What It Means

For a homebuyer, the practical implication is that the cost of building itself is not the main reason Irish homes are expensive. The cost that distinguishes Ireland is land, planning delays, infrastructure connection, and the thin pipeline of ready-to-build sites — not the price of the bricks, concrete, and labour that go into the building. A 100-square-metre new home in Ireland takes less income to build than the same home in Germany or Denmark. What is scarce, and therefore expensive, is a serviced site with permission.

For policymakers, the finding reframes the lever selection. Reducing construction costs — through simpler rules, mass modular construction, or procurement reform — would help on the margin, but it would not by itself move Ireland from building 30,000 homes a year to the 44,000 or 50,000 the country's demographics require. What does move that number is any combination of faster planning decisions, faster infrastructure upgrades, more labour, and more shovel-ready sites. The housing shortage is, on the evidence, a supply problem with a cost backdrop, not a cost problem.

## How We Did It

The analysis used the [Eurostat quarterly construction producer price index for residential buildings](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/sts_copi_q/default/table) across ten European Union countries and the United Kingdom from 2015 through 2025, together with the harmonised [Eurostat price level index for residential buildings](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/prc_ppp_ind/default/table), which produces a single level comparison across countries using the same methodology. These two series were cross-checked against industry-source absolute building costs in euros per square metre and against median household incomes. The study ranked countries on cumulative growth, fitted a panel model that allows each country its own trajectory, detected structural breaks at the pandemic and Ukraine shocks, and grouped countries by cluster analysis. All data is publicly available and harmonised by Eurostat; no synthetic data was used.
